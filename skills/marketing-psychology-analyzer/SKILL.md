# Marketing Psychology Analyzer

Analyze and optimize marketing content using proven psychology frameworks. This skill applies principles from four foundational psychology books to evaluate messaging, positioning, ads, and campaigns, identifying what psychological levers are being pulled, what's missing, and how to improve response rates.

---

## How to Use This Skill

When given marketing content to analyze, follow this framework:

### Step 1: Identify the Content Type
Classify what you're analyzing:
- Ad copy (social, search, display)
- Landing page / website copy
- Email (cold outreach, nurture, promotional)
- Positioning / messaging framework
- Campaign concept / strategy
- Sales collateral / one-pager
- Social media post
- CTA or offer

### Step 2: Run the Psychology Audit
Evaluate the content against ALL SIX sections below. For each principle, assess:
- **Present?** Is this principle being used? (Yes / Partially / No)
- **How?** If present, how is it being applied?
- **Opportunity:** If missing or weak, what specific change would activate it?
- **Risk:** Are any principles being misused or creating negative effects?

### Step 3: Deliver the Analysis
Structure your output as:
1. **Quick Verdict:** 2-3 sentence summary of what's working and what's not
2. **Psychology Scorecard:** Rate each of the 6 principle categories (Strong / Moderate / Weak / Missing)
3. **Emotion & Incentive Map:** What emotions and motivations does this content tap into?
4. **Top 3 Optimization Recommendations:** Specific, actionable changes ranked by likely impact
5. **Rewritten Example:** If appropriate, provide an optimized version of the key section

## SECTION 1: Cialdini's 6 Principles of Influence

Source: *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* by Robert Cialdini

These are the six universal principles that drive human compliance. Every piece of marketing content should consciously leverage at least 2-3 of these.

### 1.1 Reciprocity
**Principle:** People feel obligated to return favors. When you give something first, people feel compelled to give back.

**In Marketing:**
- Lead with value before asking for anything (free guides, tools, assessments, templates)
- Free trials and samples create obligation to purchase
- Generous content creates goodwill that converts later
- The "BUG" technique: let prospects try products at home, and they feel obligated to buy

**Audit Questions:**
- Does this content give something valuable before asking?
- Is there a clear value exchange that triggers reciprocity?
- Are we creating a sense of indebtedness?

**Watch Out For:**
- Gifts that feel manipulative backfire. They must feel genuine.
- The rejection-then-retreat technique: start with a large ask, then retreat to the real (smaller) ask. The contrast makes the smaller ask seem reasonable.

### 1.2 Commitment & Consistency
**Principle:** Once people take a stand or make a commitment (especially publicly or in writing), they behave consistently with that commitment.

**In Marketing:**
- Start with small asks that lead to larger ones (foot-in-the-door)
- Get prospects to self-identify: "Are you the kind of leader who...?"
- Written commitments are more powerful than verbal ones
- Public commitments are stronger than private ones
- Have customers fill out their own order forms (ownership of the decision)
- Low-ball technique: get commitment first, then reveal full scope

**Audit Questions:**
- Does this content invite a small initial commitment?
- Are we helping the prospect self-identify with our solution?
- Does the CTA feel like a natural next step (consistent with prior micro-commitments)?

**Watch Out For:**
- If the initial commitment feels forced, it creates reactance instead of compliance
- Commitments must feel voluntary to create internal consistency pressure

### 1.3 Social Proof
**Principle:** People look to others' behavior to determine correct action, especially under uncertainty. We assume an action is more correct when others are doing it.

**In Marketing:**
- Customer testimonials, case studies, logos, and counts ("10,000+ companies trust us")
- Specific numbers outperform vague claims ("reduced churn by 23%" vs "improved retention")
- Similar-other proof is most powerful: show prospects like them succeeding
- Best-seller labels, "most popular" badges, and trending indicators
- User-generated content and community activity

**Audit Questions:**
- Is there evidence that others (especially similar others) have chosen this?
- Are testimonials specific and credible (not "Consumers From Mars")?
- Does the proof match the target audience's identity?

**Watch Out For:**
- Fake or obviously manufactured social proof triggers suspicion
- When few people have adopted something, avoid highlighting the number. Use quality over quantity.
- Social proof of inaction is dangerous ("most people haven't signed up yet" = permission to not sign up)

### 1.4 Authority
**Principle:** People defer to experts and authority figures, often automatically. Titles, credentials, and trappings of authority trigger compliance.

**In Marketing:**
- Expert endorsements, analyst mentions (Gartner, Forrester)
- Author credentials, years of experience, certifications
- Industry awards and recognition
- Data-backed claims with cited sources
- Professional presentation (design quality signals competence)
- "As featured in" media logos

**Audit Questions:**
- Does this content establish credibility and expertise?
- Are claims supported by data, research, or expert opinion?
- Does the visual presentation signal authority?

**Watch Out For:**
- Authority without substance feels hollow
- Beware the Milgram effect: people follow authority even against their own judgment. Use this power responsibly.

### 1.5 Liking
**Principle:** People prefer to say yes to those they know and like. Liking is driven by: physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, familiarity, and association.

**In Marketing:**
- Mirror your audience's language, values, and identity
- Show you understand their problems (empathy = similarity)
- Use real human faces and names (not stock photos)
- Associate your brand with things your audience already likes
- The "we" pronoun: connect your brand to shared victories
- Compliment your audience's sophistication or ambition

**Audit Questions:**
- Does this content feel like it's from someone who understands the audience?
- Is there warmth and personality, or does it feel corporate and distant?
- Are we leveraging similarity (same industry, same challenges, same values)?

**Watch Out For:**
- Flattery that feels insincere backfires
- Dissimilarity in language or values creates unconscious distance
- Good Cop / Bad Cop dynamic: positioning against a common enemy creates liking through shared adversity

### 1.6 Scarcity
**Principle:** People assign more value to opportunities when they are less available. Loss aversion is stronger than desire for gain.

**In Marketing:**
- Limited-time offers and deadlines ("Offer expires Friday")
- Limited quantity ("Only 5 spots remaining")
- Exclusive access ("By invitation only", "Beta program")
- Loss framing: "Don't miss out" > "Sign up now"
- Information scarcity: exclusive insights, gated content
- Newly scarce is more powerful than always scarce

**Audit Questions:**
- Is there urgency or exclusivity in the offer?
- Is the scarcity genuine? (Fake scarcity destroys trust)
- Are we framing the cost of inaction, not just the benefit of action?

**Watch Out For:**
- Artificial scarcity that's obviously manufactured creates cynicism
- Psychological reactance: when people feel their freedom is threatened, they push back. Scarcity must feel natural, not coercive.
- Censored or restricted information becomes MORE desirable (the "banned book" effect)

## SECTION 2: Carnegie's Principles of Influence & Rapport

Source: *How to Win Friends and Influence People* by Dale Carnegie

These principles govern interpersonal influence and are critical for messaging that builds trust and motivates action.

### 2.1 Never Criticize, Condemn, or Complain
**In Marketing:**
- Don't attack the prospect's current approach or vendors directly
- Frame the problem as external, not their fault
- "The old way doesn't work anymore" > "You're doing it wrong"
- People are creatures of emotion, not logic. Lead with empathy.

### 2.2 Give Honest, Sincere Appreciation
**In Marketing:**
- Acknowledge the prospect's expertise and effort
- "You already know..." validates their intelligence
- Celebrate their ambition before presenting the solution
- Appreciation must be genuine. People detect insincerity instantly.

### 2.3 Arouse an Eager Want
**In Marketing:**
- Frame everything in terms of what the PROSPECT wants, not what you're selling
- "What's in it for them?" should drive every sentence
- Find out what they really crave and show them how to get it
- People don't care about your product. They care about their problem.

### 2.4 Talk in Terms of the Other Person's Interests
**In Marketing:**
- Use "you" more than "we"
- Lead with their pain points, not your features
- Mirror their exact language from reviews, calls, forums
- Ask: "How would I react if I were in their shoes?"

### 2.5 Make the Other Person Feel Important
**In Marketing:**
- Give them a fine reputation to live up to: "Leaders like you..."
- Acknowledge their role and responsibility
- Make them the hero of the story, not your product

### 2.6 Let Them Think It Was Their Idea
**In Marketing:**
- Guide with questions, not declarations: "Have you considered...?"
- Present options that lead to your solution organically
- Discovery-led content > prescriptive selling

### 2.7 Begin in a Friendly Way
**In Marketing:**
- Warm, conversational tone > corporate jargon
- Start with connection before pitch
- Even in cold outreach: lead with genuine interest or insight

### 2.8 Get the Other Person Saying "Yes" Immediately
**In Marketing:**
- Open with statements your audience will agree with
- "If you're like most B2B marketers, you've noticed..."
- Build a chain of agreement before the ask
- The Socratic method: lead through questions to your conclusion

### 2.9 Use Encouragement: Make Faults Seem Easy to Correct
**In Marketing:**
- Position the change as simple: "One small shift..."
- Reduce perceived effort and risk
- Show that others like them have made the same transition easily

## SECTION 3: Gladwell's Thin-Slicing & First Impressions

Source: *Blink: The Power of Thinking Without Thinking* by Malcolm Gladwell

### 3.1 Thin-Slicing: Snap Judgments Matter
**Principle:** People make rapid, unconscious assessments based on very thin slices of information. These snap judgments are often remarkably accurate, and they happen whether we want them to or not.

**In Marketing:**
- First impressions of your ad, landing page, or email happen in milliseconds
- Visual design, headline, and opening line carry disproportionate weight
- People decide "is this for me?" before they read the details
- Cluttered, confusing layouts trigger unconscious rejection

**Audit Questions:**
- What impression does someone form in the first 2 seconds?
- Does the visual hierarchy guide the eye to the key message?
- Is the value proposition instantly clear?

### 3.2 Information Overload Kills Decisions
**Principle:** "We have come to confuse information with understanding." More data does not equal better decisions. Excessive information creates paralysis.

**In Marketing:**
- Don't overwhelm with features. Lead with 1-3 key benefits.
- Simplify the decision path (fewer options = more conversions)
- Remove unnecessary form fields, steps, and choices
- "Thin-slice" your own content: what survives a 5-second scan?

### 3.3 The Power of the Adaptive Unconscious
**Principle:** Our unconscious mind processes vast amounts of information and produces "gut feelings" that are often correct. But these can be disrupted by overthinking.

**In Marketing:**
- Emotional resonance > rational argument for initial engagement
- Don't force prospects to over-analyze. Create intuitive appeal.
- Trust signals work subconsciously (design quality, social proof, authority markers)
- The "feeling" of your brand matters as much as the facts

### 3.4 Context Shapes Perception
**Principle:** The environment and framing dramatically change how we evaluate the same information.

**In Marketing:**
- Where your ad appears affects how it's perceived
- Premium placement = premium perception
- Contrast effects: show the before/after, the competitor comparison
- The "setup property" technique: show an inferior option first to make your offering shine

## SECTION 4: Schwartz's Paradox of Choice

Source: *The Paradox of Choice: Why More Is Less* by Barry Schwartz

### 4.1 Choice Overload Causes Paralysis
**Principle:** When people face too many options, they often choose nothing. The famous jam study: 6 varieties = 30% purchase rate; 24 varieties = 3% purchase rate.

**In Marketing:**
- Limit options on landing pages (1 primary CTA, not 5)
- Tiered pricing works because it simplifies: Good / Better / Best
- "Most Popular" badges reduce decision fatigue
- Feature comparison tables should highlight differences, not enumerate everything
- Remove unnecessary choices from the conversion path

**Audit Questions:**
- How many decisions are we asking the prospect to make?
- Can we reduce options without reducing value?
- Is there a clear recommended path?

### 4.2 Maximizers vs. Satisficers
**Principle:** "Maximizers" exhaustively search for the best option and are less satisfied. "Satisficers" choose the first option that meets their criteria and are happier with their choice.

**In Marketing:**
- Help maximizers feel confident: comparison content, detailed specs, social proof
- Help satisficers decide fast: clear recommendations, "best for" labels
- Position your product as the "good enough" that exceeds expectations
- Reduce post-purchase doubt with confirmation messaging

### 4.3 Opportunity Cost & Regret
**Principle:** The more options available, the more people dwell on missed opportunities. Anticipated regret prevents decisions.

**In Marketing:**
- Reduce perceived risk: money-back guarantees, free trials, easy cancellation
- "You can always switch later" reduces commitment anxiety
- Frame the bigger regret: "What happens if you do nothing?"
- Make decisions feel reversible to reduce regret aversion

### 4.4 The Escalation of Expectations
**Principle:** More choices raise expectations. When expectations are sky-high, even good outcomes feel disappointing.

**In Marketing:**
- Set clear, specific expectations (not vague promises)
- Underpromise and overdeliver
- "Here's exactly what you'll get" > "Transform your business!"
- Manage expectations at every stage of the funnel

### 4.5 The Comparison Trap
**Principle:** We evaluate our choices relative to alternatives, not in absolute terms. Social comparison drives dissatisfaction.

**In Marketing:**
- Control the comparison frame: define what you're compared against
- "vs. hiring an agency" or "vs. building in-house". Choose your foil.
- Don't let prospects compare on dimensions where you lose
- The contrast principle (from Cialdini): anchor high, then present your price

### 4.6 Choice and Autonomy
**Principle:** Some choice is essential for wellbeing, but too much choice becomes a burden. The best approach: curate, don't eliminate.

**In Marketing:**
- Guided selling: "Based on your needs, we recommend..."
- Quiz-style or assessment-based funnels that personalize recommendations
- "We did the research so you don't have to"
- Position yourself as the curator/guide, not just another option

## SECTION 5: Emotion & Motivation Map

When analyzing content, identify which core emotions and motivations are being targeted:

### Primary Emotions in B2B
| Emotion | Trigger | Example Copy |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Fear of missing out (FOMO) | Scarcity, social proof | "Your competitors are already using this" |
| Fear of failure | Loss aversion, authority | "Don't be the team that missed the shift" |
| Desire for status | Authority, social proof | "Join the top 5% of marketers who..." |
| Need for certainty | Social proof, authority | "Proven by 500+ enterprise teams" |
| Desire for ease | Choice reduction, thin-slicing | "One platform. Everything you need." |
| Belonging | Liking, social proof | "Built by marketers, for marketers" |
| Autonomy / control | Choice, commitment | "You decide. No lock-in contracts." |
| Guilt / obligation | Reciprocity | Free value that creates indebtedness |

### Primary Motivations
- **Gain:** What will they get? (revenue, efficiency, status)
- **Logic:** Why does this make rational sense? (ROI, data, proof)
- **Fear:** What will they lose by not acting? (competitive disadvantage, wasted spend, missed opportunity)

The most effective marketing content activates all three: Gain + Logic + Fear.

## SECTION 6: Anti-Patterns (What NOT to Do)

### Psychology Violations That Kill Conversion

1. **Information Overload:** Too many features, too much text, too many CTAs. Triggers choice paralysis (Schwartz).

2. **Criticizing the Prospect:** Making them feel stupid for their current approach. Triggers defensiveness (Carnegie).

3. **Fake Scarcity:** Countdown timers that reset, "limited spots" that never run out. Destroys trust (Cialdini).

4. **No Social Proof:** Asking people to take a leap of faith with no evidence others have done it. Triggers uncertainty (Cialdini).

5. **Feature Dumping:** Listing 47 features instead of 3 benefits. Overwhelms the adaptive unconscious (Gladwell).

6. **Self-Centered Copy:** "We're proud to announce..." / "Our platform offers..." Nobody cares about you. Talk about THEM (Carnegie).

7. **Forced Commitment:** Aggressive popups, dark patterns, mandatory demos. Triggers psychological reactance (Schwartz/Cialdini).

8. **Vague Authority:** "Trusted by leading companies" without naming them. Weak social proof is worse than none.

9. **Ignoring Loss Aversion:** Only talking about gains. People are 2x more motivated by avoiding loss than achieving gain.

10. **No Clear Next Step:** Content that educates but doesn't guide. Every piece must have one obvious action.
