# Cialdini's Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion - Quick Reference

## The 6 Weapons of Influence

### 1. Reciprocity: The Old Give and Take
- People repay in kind. Give first, then ask.
- Uninvited favors still create obligation (Hare Krishna flower technique)
- The Amway "BUG": leave free samples at home, and customers buy out of obligation
- **Rejection-then-retreat**: Start with a large request. When refused, retreat to a smaller request. The contrast makes the smaller ask seem reasonable AND the prospect feels the concession must be reciprocated.
- Works even when the gift/favor is unwanted
- Power: People hate feeling indebted. They'll comply just to clear the debt.

### 2. Commitment & Consistency: Hobgoblins of the Mind
- Once people commit (especially in writing or publicly), they act consistently
- **Foot-in-the-door**: Small commitment leads to larger compliance later
- Written commitments > verbal. Public > private. Effortful > easy.
- The toy company trick: advertise a toy before Christmas, understock it, parents commit to buying it, stock it in January. Parents buy it to stay consistent.
- The Chinese POW technique: small essay contests with tiny prizes. Prisoners who wrote pro-Communist statements internalized the belief.
- **Low-ball technique**: Get commitment first, then change the deal. People stick with their commitment.
- Having customers fill out their own forms increases follow-through
- Self-image: once someone sees themselves as "the kind of person who...", they act accordingly

### 3. Social Proof: Truths Are Us
- We determine correct behavior by observing what others do
- Most powerful under uncertainty and when the "others" are similar to us
- Canned laughter works despite being obvious. It triggers automatic response.
- The Werther Effect: publicized suicides cause more suicides (people imitate similar others)
- Bystander effect: more observers = less individual action (diffusion of responsibility)
- **Pluralistic ignorance**: when everyone looks calm, each individual assumes nothing is wrong
- O'Connor's film study: showing withdrawn children a film of children joining groups permanently changed their behavior after just one viewing
- Works for marketing: "10,000 customers" / "most popular plan" / "trending now"

### 4. Social Proof: Truths Are Us (continued)
- Best-seller labels increase sales (the Matthew Effect)
- Similar-other proof is strongest: show prospects like them succeeding
- Manufactured social proof ("Consumers From Mars") triggers suspicion
- When behavior is public and observable, social proof is most powerful

### 5. Liking: The Friendly Thief
- We comply with people we like. Five factors drive liking:
  - **Physical attractiveness** (halo effect: attractive = competent, honest, talented)
  - **Similarity** (dress, background, interests, language)
  - **Compliments** (even obvious flattery works more than expected)
  - **Familiarity** (mere exposure increases liking, via contact & cooperation)
  - **Association** (we like messengers who bring good news; brand + celebrity)
- Tupperware party model: buy from a friend, not a company
- Car salespeople mirror customer interests found in trade-in vehicles
- Good Cop / Bad Cop: contrast makes Good Cop seem likable and trustworthy
- "We" vs "They": fans say "we won" but "they lost". Association with success.

### 6. Authority: Directed Deference
- People obey authority automatically, even against their judgment
- Milgram experiment: 65% shocked to maximum voltage when told to by authority figure
- **Titles** affect perception: "Doctor" or "Professor" changes how claims are received
- **Clothing** signals authority: uniforms, suits trigger automatic compliance
- **Trappings**: expensive cars, prestigious offices, professional design
- Bank examiner con: professional appearance + authoritative presentation = compliance
- Nurses followed clearly incorrect doctor orders 95% of the time (Hofling study)
- **Defense**: Ask "Is this authority truly an expert? How truthful can we expect them to be here?"

### 7. Scarcity: The Rule of the Few
- Less available = more desirable. Loss aversion > desire for gain.
- **Limited number** technique: "Only 3 left"
- **Deadline** technique: "Offer ends Friday"
- **Exclusive information**: "I have information that isn't publicly available..."
- Newly scarce > always scarce (the cookie jar experiment: cookies from a jar that went from 10 to 2 were rated tastier than cookies from a jar that always had 2)
- **Psychological reactance**: when freedom is threatened, we want it more (the "Romeo and Juliet effect", where parental interference increases romantic feelings)
- Censorship increases desire for and belief in the censored information
- Competition for scarce resources intensifies desire (auction dynamics)
- Richard's used car technique: schedule multiple buyers at the same time. Competition + scarcity = higher prices.

## The Contrast Principle
- Runs through all 6 weapons
- We evaluate things relative to what came before
- Real estate agents show "dump" houses first to make target house look great
- Car dealers negotiate price first, then add accessories. Each seems small by comparison.
- Always present the expensive option before the affordable one
